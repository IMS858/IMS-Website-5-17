What actually moves the needle with food

The nutrition conversation gets loud fast. Here's the short list of things that reliably matter for adults who train, in rough order.

May 2026 · 6 min read

I get asked about food constantly, and the honest answer is less exciting than people want. A handful of things do most of the work. Everything after that is refinement, and a lot of what gets sold as essential is refinement being marketed as foundation.

Worth saying up front: I'm a coach, not a dietitian. This is general information about eating to support training, not nutrition advice for your situation. If you have a medical condition, take medication, or are managing something specific, that's a conversation for a registered dietitian or your physician.

1. Protein, and enough of it

If you're training for strength and you change one thing, make it this. Most adults I meet are eating noticeably less protein than their training would justify, and it shows up as slow recovery and disappointing progress despite real effort in the gym.

Practical version: get a real protein source into every meal rather than trying to make up a deficit at dinner. Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes — the source matters less than the total and the distribution across the day.

2. Mostly whole foods, most of the time

Food that resembles what it was is easier to eat sensibly. It carries more fibre and micronutrients per calorie, it's more filling, and it doesn't require you to track anything to end up in a reasonable place.

This isn't a moral position and it doesn't need to be absolute. "Mostly, most of the time" is doing real work in that sentence. A pattern you can hold for years beats a stricter one you abandon in March.

3. Enough total food

Undereating is at least as common as overeating in the people I coach, particularly among those who've spent years dieting. If you're training hard, sleeping badly, and constantly sore, insufficient fuel is worth ruling out before you go looking for anything more complicated.

4. Water and sleep, which are not food but decide how food lands

Neither is interesting. Both outperform every supplement I've ever been asked about. If sleep is genuinely poor, fixing that will do more for your training than any change to your grocery list.

On organic, and on how I'd think about it

Clients ask whether organic is worth it. My honest read: if buying organic produce means you eat more vegetables because you feel better about them, that's a real benefit and you should do it. If the cost means you buy fewer vegetables, that trade is going the wrong way. Eating more plants is the well-supported part; the organic premium is a smaller and more contested variable sitting on top of it.

The same reasoning applies to a lot of food-quality questions. Ask what the change does to your overall pattern. A choice that makes you eat better in general is worth more than one that's theoretically optimal and practically annoying.

Why I'm not writing the toxin post

I get asked about glyphosate, heavy metals, and fragrance chemicals often enough that it's worth addressing directly rather than ignoring.

These are real subjects with real research attached, and also subjects where the confident version circulating online runs well ahead of what's actually established. Regulatory bodies disagree with each other on some of it. The dose and exposure route matter enormously and usually get dropped from the summary. And "detox" as it's typically sold — a protocol that clears accumulated toxins — isn't a thing your liver and kidneys need help with from a supplement.

I'm not going to tell you those questions don't matter. I am going to tell you that I'm a strength and mobility coach, that claims about chemical exposure and health outcomes are outside what I'm qualified to make, and that a studio which promises not to make medical claims shouldn't start making them in its blog. If this is your area of interest, take it to someone whose actual expertise it is.

What I can say with confidence is the boring list above. It's less satisfying than a villain, and it works.

The one-sentence version

Eat enough, eat enough protein, favour food that looks like food, sleep, and keep it up long enough for it to count.
